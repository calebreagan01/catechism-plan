This plan is meant for a person in the first years of following Jesus. It walks through the whole Bible in 900 days, stops now and then to teach the beliefs of the Christian faith, and tries to show you how to read Scripture for yourself. The name is a humble one. A catechism is a tool for teaching new believers what the church believes, and that is the job I hope this plan does.

Before you spend two and a half years with it, you should know who wrote it and where he is standing. Every teacher stands somewhere, and a guide that pretends to stand nowhere is not being honest with you.

## Where I Am Coming From

I come from the evangelical tradition, with Reformed instincts. I believe the Bible is the word of God, true and trustworthy from beginning to end. I believe in one God in three persons, in the full deity and full humanity of Jesus, in his death in the place of sinners and his bodily resurrection, and in salvation by grace alone through faith alone in Christ alone. My teaching leans toward the sovereignty of God in salvation and the sufficiency of Christ, and I do not want to hide that.

The framework behind the plan is called progressive covenantalism, which I learned chiefly from Peter Gentry and Stephen Wellum in their book Kingdom through Covenant. The idea is simple to state. The Bible tells one story, and God tells it through a series of covenants: with creation and Adam, with Noah, with Abraham, with Israel at Sinai, with David, and finally the new covenant in Christ. Each covenant builds on the one before it and points ahead to the next. Jesus fulfills them all.

That idea explains the shape of the plan. Year 1, Promise and Shadow, moves through the covenants one at a time. Year 2, Fulfillment, follows Jesus, the church, and the new creation. Many of the units carry the names of covenants. The Connection section of a daily guide asks how a passage leads to Christ. The middle question on each Sabbath day is always about him. These are choices that come straight from the framework.

Not every Christian reads the Bible this way. Some hold that God has one covenant of grace unfolding through history, with Israel and the church closely joined. Others distinguish sharply between God’s purposes for Israel and for the church. Those traditions have produced faithful, Bible-loving people, and they will sometimes read a passage differently than I do. Where it matters I have tried to say so. Where I have not, you may need to notice it yourself.

I have also tried to avoid the private vocabulary of any one camp. The goal is accessible depth: enough substance to feed a growing believer, without requiring you to join a side before you can read a page.

## Decisions You Should Know About

A plan like this makes hundreds of choices on the reader’s behalf. Most are invisible. These are the ones I think you should see.

**The story is organized by covenant.** The units follow the covenants, not the order of the books in your Bible. That is a way of reading the Bible, not the only way.

**The reading order is a decision.** The plan is not in canonical order. Job sits between Abraham and the Exodus, following the common view that its setting is patriarchal, though the book does not date itself and scholars differ. The Psalms are woven through the whole plan. The Wisdom books come after David and Solomon. The Gospels are read in the order Matthew, Luke, John, and Mark. Hebrews comes before the General Epistles and Revelation. Other orders are reasonable, and where the date or authorship of a book is debated, the Books of the Bible section of the first appendix says so.

**Christ is the center of every part.** Jesus taught that the Law, the Prophets, and the Psalms all speak of him (Luke 24:44). I have read the Old Testament that way throughout. Some readers will feel that I find Christ in places where the original author may not have seen him clearly. I have tried to lean on how the New Testament itself reads the Old, and to be modest where it does not.

**The doctrinal pauses reflect my judgment.** The 44 doctrinal days cover what I think a new believer most needs, plus the teachings the story itself raises. Where Christians have long agreed, I state it plainly. Where faithful Christians differ, as with the mode and subjects of baptism, I try to describe the positions fairly and say that they differ. I will not always have kept my own view out of it.

**The weekly rhythm is built into the calendar.** Six days of reading are followed by a day of rest, and the unit boundaries bend around that rhythm. The rest day falls on Saturday only because the plan begins on a Sunday. How Christians should keep the Sabbath or the Lord’s Day is a question on which they differ, and the plan does not settle it.

**Every guide is a selection.** Each daily guide is short, and it leaves out far more than it includes. Choosing what to notice in a passage is already interpretation. Another teacher would choose differently, and the passage is always richer than the guide.

**The translation is the English Standard Version.** The guides refer to the ESV. Any faithful translation will serve you, and reading in more than one is a good habit.

## What I Hold Firmly and What I Hold Loosely

It would be a mistake to treat every sentence in this plan as equally certain. I hold some things with my whole weight and others with an open hand.

I hold these firmly, because the church has confessed them from the beginning and the Bible is plain about them:

- The Scriptures are the word of God and our final authority.
- There is one God, eternally Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.
- Jesus is fully God and fully man, who died for sinners and rose bodily from the dead.
- We are saved by grace through faith in Christ, not by our own works.
- Jesus will return, and the dead will be raised.
I hold these more loosely, because faithful Christians have disagreed about them and I may be wrong:

- How the covenants relate Israel and the church in every detail.
- The timing and shape of the end of the age.
- The mode and subjects of baptism, and how the Sabbath carries over.
- The date, authorship, and setting of some of the books of the Bible.
- The details of church government and practice.

## A Flawed Teacher and a Faithful Word

I am wholeheartedly persuaded that God’s word is true. I am not persuaded that I have understood all of it rightly. The daily guides are not Scripture. They are the work of one flawed person, still learning, trying to explain a book that is far greater than he is. I will have made mistakes of fact, of emphasis, and of judgment, and some of them I cannot see.

Because this plan is written to shape what you believe, I owe you a plain warning. A reader who trusts the guide more than the text has been led astray, however good the guide. If anything here disagrees with the passage you are reading, trust the passage. Luke praises the believers in Berea because they received the word eagerly and examined the Scriptures daily to see if what Paul said was so (Acts 17:11). Please read this plan the same way.

Read it with other people. Bring your questions and your disagreements to your own pastor or a mature Christian who knows you. They are in a better position than I am to correct me, and to help you where my tradition and yours part ways. Where I have gotten something wrong, I would rather be corrected than repeated.

This plan stands on the work of many teachers I could never list, whose books, sermons, and commentaries taught me to read the Bible. Whatever is good in it belongs to them and to the Lord who gave them. Whatever is mistaken is mine.

My hope is not that you will agree with me. It is that you will love the Bible, know Jesus, and become a person who can open the Scriptures and read them for yourself. I pray what the psalmist prayed: Open my eyes, that I may behold wondrous things out of your law (Psalm 119:18).

*Caleb*
