Most of the Bible tells you what God has done. The Psalms are where God’s people answer him. For that reason the plan does not save the Psalms for one stretch of the calendar. It weaves them through all 900 days, so that after a long run of narrative, law, prophecy, or letter there is a day when you stop reading about people who prayed and pray alongside them.

The Psalms earn that place. They are among the most quoted books of the Old Testament in the New. Jesus taught that the Psalms speak of him (Luke 24:44). After the Last Supper he and his disciples sang a hymn (Matthew 26:30), traditionally the Hallel, Psalms 113 to 118. On the cross he prayed the opening of Psalm 22 and, at the end, a line from Psalm 31 (Matthew 27:46; Luke 23:46). A plan meant to teach new believers how to speak to God, and not only what to believe about him, needs the Psalms at its heart.

## Five Kinds of Day

The plan has five kinds of day. The Psalms are one of them, and the other four are built around them, so it helps to see how they fit together.

- **Reading days.** 472 days. The main readings that carry the story, the law, the prophets, and the letters.
- **Psalm days.** 233 days. One psalm, or a short pair, set beside the surrounding reading.
- **Doctrinal pauses.** 44 days. A passage or two studied for one teaching of the faith.
- **Unit reviews.** 25 in all. Twenty-two take a day of their own, and three are combined with a Sabbath.
- **Sabbath rest.** 129, one for every week. In 126 the day is only rest, and in three it is combined with a review.
Psalm days are the second largest category, and on average they fall on a little under two days of each week. The plan was designed around that rhythm: narrative days carry the story forward, and psalm days let you stop and respond.

## The Psalms by the Numbers

- **Share of the plan.** 233 of 900 days, about one in four.
- **Coverage.** All 150 psalms are read at least once.
- **Different readings.** 174. Long psalms are divided (Psalm 119 into several sections, and Psalms 18, 22, 78, 89, 104, and 107 into two) and 48 of the readings come back in a new setting.
- **Paired days.** 16 days read two short psalms together.
- **Typical length.** About 16 verses (the middle reading is 13), against about 60 verses for a reading day.
- **Year 1 and Year 2.** 158 psalm days in Year 1 and 75 in Year 2. The Old Testament is where most of the Psalms were written, and where they most often sit beside a story.
- **The largest block.** Unit 9, The Davidic Covenant, has 31 psalm days out of 67.

## What a Psalm Day Looks Like

A psalm day has the same seven parts as any reading day, so you always know what to expect. The differences are in emphasis. Because a psalm is a poem, the guide spends less time on events and more on structure, imagery, and the turns of thought. Setting the Scene gives the author, the heading, and the occasion if one is known. Observation points out repeated words, shifts of mood, and the line where a complaint becomes trust. Connection shows where the psalm points to Christ or where the New Testament quotes it. The Prayer at the end is only an echo. The psalm itself is the prayer.

The reading is short on purpose. A psalm is meant to be read aloud, slowly, and read again. You are not meant to finish it so much as stay with it.

## Five Jobs the Psalms Do

**They answer the story.** Narrative shows what people did and what God did. The Psalms give the inner voice. After David’s sin with Bathsheba and Nathan’s rebuke, the plan gives you Psalms 32 and 51. After Absalom’s revolt, it gives you Psalm 63. You do not only read that David repented. You hear how he prayed.

**They interpret the story.** Some psalms retell Israel’s history as worship: Psalms 78, 105, 106, 114, 135, and 136. Others were written in the middle of a crisis you are reading about. Psalms 46, 48, and 76 stand beside Sennacherib’s siege of Jerusalem. Psalm 90, attributed to Moses, comes among the first chapters of Numbers. Psalm 95 warns against a hard heart just after Israel’s unbelief at Kadesh in Numbers 13 and 14. The Psalter becomes a commentary written by the people who lived it.

**They carry Christ through the Old Testament.** The New Testament leans on the Psalms to explain who Jesus is. In Unit 19 you read Psalms 2, 16, and 110 just after the apostles preach and pray from them. In Unit 22, you read Psalm 68, which Paul quotes in Ephesians 4 to describe the ascended Christ. In Unit 23, Psalms 95, 40, 110, and 22 sit beside Hebrews, which quotes all four. The psalm gives the first sound, and the New Testament gives the full chord.

**They teach the whole range of prayer.** The Psalms contain praise, thanksgiving, confession, lament, trust, wisdom, and anger. A new believer often assumes that only polite prayers are allowed. The Psalms say otherwise. They are included in all their range, and the hard ones are not skipped. Psalms 58, 109, and 137 call on God to judge those who do violence. The guide reads them honestly, as the cry of the oppressed handed over to God, and then in the light of Christ, who prayed for his enemies and who will judge the world in righteousness.

**They set the pace.** Heavy stretches of law, genealogy, and prophecy are followed by a short day of poetry. That keeps the plan humane. It is also a lesson in its own right: Scripture is meant to be prayed as well as studied.

## How the Psalms Were Placed

No psalm was dropped in at random. Each one was set beside a reading it illuminates, using four kinds of placement.

**Beside the event.** The psalm is paired with the moment it most naturally belongs to, such as Psalms 32 and 51 after Nathan confronts David, or Psalm 63 after Absalom’s revolt.

**Beside the theme.** The psalm takes up what the surrounding books are saying. Beside Job, the plan places the psalms of the suffering righteous: Psalms 10, 13, 22, 73, and 88. Beside Proverbs, it places Psalms 19 and 119 on the word of God. Beside Ecclesiastes, it places Psalms 49 and 73 on wealth and the brevity of life. Beside the Song of Solomon, it places Psalm 45.

**Beside the quotation.** When a New Testament author quotes a psalm, the plan places that psalm close by, so that you meet the original in its own setting before you see how it is used. This is one reason the Psalms cluster beside Acts and Hebrews.

**At the edges.** Psalms 1 and 2 are the front door of the Psalter, and they open the first run of David’s psalms in Unit 9 (Days 237 and 239), return beside the Sermon on the Mount and the Transfiguration in Unit 15, and in Unit 25 as Revelation opens and as the nations rage. The last two psalm days of the plan, Days 897 and 898, are Psalm 72:18-19, the doxology that ends the second book of the Psalter, and Psalm 150, the final psalm of the Psalter itself.

## Why Some Psalms Come Back

You will meet some psalms more than once, and that is on purpose. A psalm read beside a different story means something different. Psalm 2 appears four times: on Day 239 beside David’s throne, on Day 586 beside the Father’s voice at the Transfiguration, on Day 696 beside the prayer of the church in Acts 4, and on Day 884 as Revelation shows the nations raging. Psalm 22 appears five times: twice beside Job, then beside David, beside Isaiah’s servant, and beside Hebrews. Psalm 110 appears five times, once on a doctrinal day.

When a psalm comes back, the guide gives it a new angle suited to the new setting. If you think, “I have read this before,” you are right. Read it again, and notice what is different around it. Repetition is how the Bible itself teaches, and a psalm you already know is an old friend and not filler.

## The Psalter’s Five Books

The Psalms are gathered into five books: Psalms 1 to 41, 42 to 72, 73 to 89, 90 to 106, and 107 to 150. Each book ends with a short burst of praise called a doxology, and Psalm 150 closes the whole collection. The plan does not follow that order, because the order it follows is the order of the story. It does touch all five books, and it ends in the Psalter’s own closing notes.

## Reading a Psalm Day Well

- Read the psalm aloud, and then read it again. These were written to be heard.
- Notice the turn. Many psalms begin in complaint and end in trust, and the turn is where the lesson is.
- Let a hard psalm be hard. Read to the end before you decide what it means.
- Pray the psalm in your own words after you read it, using its shape but your own life.
- Sing it if you can. Many hymns are psalms set to music.
- Ask the question this whole plan asks: where is Christ in this psalm?
